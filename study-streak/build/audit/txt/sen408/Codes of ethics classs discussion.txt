Codes of Ethics, Professional Associations, Laws 
Ethical dilemmas that you might encounter in your first job, startups, software projects, or AI 
ventures. 
Option 1: The boss says, “Just write the code”—who is responsible when it causes harm? 
Imagine you are a software engineer in a Nigerian fintech startup. Your manager instructs you to 
deploy an algorithm that automatically rejects loan applications. You discover that it 
disproportionately rejects applicants from certain low-income communities, but your manager says 
the system is profitable and the deadline is tomorrow. 
• Should you obey your employer, challenge the decision, or refuse to deploy the system? 
What if refusing costs you your job? 
• Does writing code make you responsible only for its technical performance, or also for its 
social consequences? 
• How would the ACM Code of Ethics and the IEEE/ACM Software Engineering Code of 
Ethics guide your decision? 
 
 
 
 
Option 2: If users click “I agree,” does that make the collection and use of their data ethical? 
A Nigerian technology company develops a free campus app that helps students find accommodation 
and share transport. The app collects location data, phone contacts, browsing behaviour, and other 
personal information. Its terms and conditions allow the company to share data with advertisers. 
Almost nobody reads the terms. 
• Is the company acting ethically simply because users agreed to its terms? What if users do not 
understand what they are agreeing to? 
• Which information does the app genuinely need, and which information is it collecting 
merely because it can? 
• How would the Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) 2023 and, where applicable, the GDPR 
affect your design decisions? 
• If you were the developer, would you challenge the business model even if the company were 
complying with the law? 
• Is a user's consent meaningful when refusing it means losing access to an essential service?

Option 3: If a practice is legal and profitable, can it still be professionally unethical? 
Imagine that your software company develops facial-recognition technology for an organisation that 
wants to monitor employees and visitors. The client insists that the system is legal and that it will 
improve security. You discover that the system performs less accurately for some demographic 
groups and that people may be monitored without their knowledge. 
• Is complying with the law sufficient to fulfil your ethical responsibilities as a software 
engineer? 
• When should a developer challenge a client's instructions, even if the client has legal 
authority to request the system? 
• Should professional codes require engineers to consider the dignity, privacy, and wellbeing of 
people affected by their products? 
• If the system is legal in Nigeria but would face stricter restrictions elsewhere, which 
standards should guide your decisions? 
• Should software engineers or computer scientists be regarded as neutral technical workers 
who simply implement decisions, or as professionals who share responsibility for the 
consequences of their work? 
Clue: See ACM's principles on public wellbeing, fairness, privacy, and professional responsibility, 
alongside applicable legal requirements (check for Nigeria) 
 
Option 4 
Why belong to a professional association if your university degree and technical skills are 
enough? 
You graduate and receive two job offers. One company rewards you for releasing products quickly, 
discourages employees from reporting ethical concerns, and provides little professional development. 
The other encourages professional review, continued learning, and participation in computing 
associations such as the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM), IEEE, or relevant Nigerian 
professional bodies. 
• What can membership in a professional association offer that a university degree, technical 
certification, or work experience cannot? 
• Can a professional association help you resist unethical instructions from an employer? How 
might a code of ethics, peer support, professional networks, and continuing education help? 
• If professional associations publish ethical codes but cannot prevent every violation, do those 
codes make a real difference? 
• What responsibilities should members accept in return for belonging to a professional 
community? 
Provocation: If you want society to trust software engineers with systems that affect people's money, 
privacy, health, and opportunities, what evidence should society have that you take your professional 
responsibilities seriously?
