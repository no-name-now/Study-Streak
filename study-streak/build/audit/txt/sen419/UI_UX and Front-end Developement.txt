Visibility vs Feedback
Visibility is passive — it's about the system's current state being 
readable at any moment (e.g. a cart badge showing "3 items" 
whether the user did anything or not). 
Feedback is reactive — it's a direct response to something the user 
just did. Clicking a button should trigger a hover state, a press 
animation, then either a success message or an error. No action 
should disappear into silence.

Hierarchy
Hierarchy helps users recognise key 
information and distinguish it from less 
important elements at a glance. Think of it 
like a book: navigational cues on every page 
remind you where you are, so you never get 
lost.

--- page 24 ---
Accessibility - Semantic HTML  
Use <button> not <div onclick>. Use <nav>, 
<main>, <h1>. Semantic elements give screen 
readers the context they need to narrate your 
UI correctly. Correct HTML is always the ﬁrst 
step — ARIA is a last resort.
--- page 25 ---
Accessibility - Color Contrast  
WCAG AA requires 4.5:1 contrast for body text 
and 3:1 for large text. Use tools like Colour 
Contrast Analyser. Never rely on color alone to 
convey information — always pair it with text or 
iconography.
--- page 26 ---
Accessibility - Keyboard Navigation 
Every interactive element must be reachable 
and operable via keyboard alone. Focus 
indicators must be clearly visible. Tab order 
must follow a logical reading sequence.
--- page 27 ---
Accessibility - ARIA Labels 
aria-label, aria-describedby, role="..." ﬁll 
semantic gaps HTML can't cover. Use them 
sparingly — correct HTML ﬁrst, ARIA only where 
HTML falls short
--- page 28 ---
Accessibility - Alt Text
Every informative image needs descriptive alt 
text. Decorative images get alt="". Complex 
charts need long descriptions or an accessible 
data table alternative.
--- page 29 ---
Accessibility - WCAG 2.2 AA
The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, 
Level AA, is the legal standard in most 
jurisdictions. Built around four principles — 
Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, 
Robust (POUR).
--- page 30 ---
Key requirements from WCAG (Web Content Accessibility 
Guidelines):
Sufficient contrast between foreground and background 
colors (≥ 4.5:1 for body text)
Alternative text for all informative images
Proper keyboard navigation for all interactive elements
Compatibility with assistive technology (screen readers, 
switch controls)
Appropriate padding and touch target sizes for motor 
accessibility

Contrast
A "Delete account" button in red on a white background 
commands immediate attention and signals danger
A secondary "Keep account" option in grey recedes — 
reducing the chance of an accidental destructive action
For critical information, higher, more jarring contrast 
commands attention; for supportive content, softer 
contrast keeps it from competing

--- page 38 ---
Proximity
Things that belong together should stay 
together. Users naturally perceive UI 
elements that are close to each other as 
related — this creates intuitive 
groupings without needing labels or 
dividers.

--- page 40 ---
Proximity
On a video player: Play, Rewind, and 
Fast-forward sit together because they 
share a function. The Quit or Close button 
lives separately — not just for aesthetics, 
but to prevent accidental clicks that would 
interrupt the experience.
--- page 41 ---
Alignment
Clean lines make designs feel professional 
and trustworthy. A strong grid system 
establishes order and visual balance — 
even when the grid itself is invisible, users 
feel its presence in how easy it is to read 
and navigate the interface.
--- page 42 ---
Alignment
Consistent alignment creates 
predictability. When elements line up with 
each other, users can scan quickly. When 
they don't, the eye stops and the brain has 
to work harder to parse the layout.
